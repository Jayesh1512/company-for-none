# Near Council — independent build-plan critique

Review date: 10 November 2026. Sources: all of `PRD.md` (104 lines), all of the original `BUILD_PLAN.md` (278 lines), `README.md`, and the repository file inventory. No applicable `AGENTS.md` or other repository instructions were found in the workspace or its ancestor directories. The repository contains planning documents, not evidence of a working product or validated demand. Original source files are preserved.

## Executive verdict: not ready for the full build

The plan is ready to support a bounded discovery and feasibility round. It is **not ready to authorize the full build or customer charging**. This is a judgment about the evidence and gates, not a finding that the idea cannot work.

The strongest choices should survive: sell a company-specific deliverable rather than celebrity imitation; use authorized contributions; distinguish agent conclusions from actual human review; keep agent handoffs and disagreement inspectable; reserve pending spending; protect company knowledge; and require an unassisted release rehearsal.

The largest investment risk is building most of the workspace before testing whether expert guidance adds value beyond ordinary AI assistance. The proposed comparison tests collaboration while holding expert guidance constant. It therefore cannot establish the reason to pay for that guidance. A second risk is treating a positioning service and a service-bound profile license as the destination without deciding whether they preserve the marketplace the user actually wants. Both are explicitly called assumptions in the original; they are not secretly undisclosed requirements. The weakness is the missing test and decision before committing to them.

The revised plan moves commercial, comparative, rights, and dependency evidence ahead of the full build; makes customer promises and exception ownership concrete; and separates supported pilot readiness from unassisted release and marketplace expansion. It proposes product choices for approval; it does not amend the PRD.

Severity means **Critical**: must be resolved before accepting money or confidential material for the affected workflow; **High**: must be resolved before the next substantial investment or release decision; **Medium**: improves a bounded pilot without blocking unrelated validation. “Blocker” below identifies the relevant stage, not a demand that every issue be solved before any work.

## Prioritized material findings

### F1 — The entry offer can become a different product without an explicit decision

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to committing the full build. **Original:** §1, line 9; §2, lines 21, 26–27; §4, lines 70, 82–83; §8, lines 273–275. **PRD:** lines 14, 32, 43–44, 55, 69–76.

**Unsupported assumption:** launch positioning is the best recurring problem, buyers will accept profile access only inside one service, and three preset agents satisfy the intended collaborative workspace. The PRD permits a narrow starting point but says the category should follow discovery; it also explicitly permits inviting an agent/service and asking for another view.

**Failure and impact:** buyers want to apply an expert's judgment to ongoing company decisions, but the product sells isolated positioning reports. A one-off service appears to work while the original marketplace and repeat-use proposition remain untested.

**Smallest change:** label the category, profile restriction, and presets as proposed pilot choices. Test profile-use expectations and real next decisions; retain member-initiated challenge and an approved service invitation within the bounded workflow. Do not build standalone licensing merely to answer this question.

**Verify:** record buyer understanding of what is licensed, expert agreement, demand lost because of the restriction, and a user decision to keep or change the entry offer before full build. Revised plan §§1–2, Phase A.

### F2 — Expensive learning is scheduled after expensive construction

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to full build; critical dependency failures block charging or confidential inputs. **Original:** §5, line 93; Phase 0, lines 107–117; Phases 1–4; Phase 5, lines 210–218.

**Unsupported assumption:** one sample and broadly “credible” dependency fit justify building the workspace, collaboration, commerce, and delivery journeys before comparing service value. The plan already starts vendor checks early; the problem is that an owned gap can remain open while substantial dependent work proceeds.

**Failure and impact:** the service is indistinguishable from an ordinary AI response, or intended buyers cannot complete the payment journey, discovered after several large phases. A team has spent heavily before learning whether the offer or purchase is viable.

**Smallest change:** move a small consented comparative service test and a target-buyer payment rehearsal ahead of the full build. Require observed evidence for critical dependencies, not a vendor feature claim or assigned gap. Permit only isolated, reversible validation while a critical gap remains.

**Verify:** a pre-build decision packet contains comparative outputs, buyer reactions, authorized contribution rights, intended buyer payment steps and recovery, company-knowledge boundaries, and an explicit pass/change/stop for each dependency. Revised plan Phases A–B and §5.

### F3 — The comparison cannot isolate expert value, and scoring can favor the seller

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to claiming differentiation or collaboration advantage. **Original:** Phase 5, lines 216–219, 228; §7, lines 258–259, 267.

**Unsupported assumption:** comparing two outputs that both use expert guidance establishes the paid expert contribution; a contributing expert is a sufficiently independent judge; varied presentation order and an equal maximum allowance control bias and effort.

**Failure and impact:** both versions would beat a weak prompt but neither beats a competent ordinary AI workflow. The expert prefers familiar language, or staff have improved the brief before both runs. The team attributes gains to the marketplace or council that actually came from prompting, preparation, or unequal work.

**Smallest change:** use three versions on the same four briefs: a competent ordinary single-agent alternative using public guidance; the same approach with authorized expert guidance; and the collaborative version using that guidance. Capture the buyer's real existing alternative. Freeze inputs and rubrics, separate preparation and editing effort, conceal production method, and define tie and rater-disagreement handling. Include customer-prepared briefs without operator rewriting before claiming independence from operator help. Use an independent qualified scorer where possible; disclose conflicts otherwise.

**Verify:** expert increment is assessed between the first two versions; collaboration increment between the latter two. Record actual cost and time, adverse outcomes, and all briefs. Human review is a separate later measurement. This small study is directional and does not establish every interaction or broad superiority. Revised plan §4 and Phases B, F.

### F4 — Recruitment and repeat demand have no bounded path to a decision

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to a credible pilot schedule and demand gate. **Original:** Phase 0, lines 105, 115; Phase 5, lines 214–215, 226–230; Phase 6, line 240.

**Unsupported assumption:** six interviews can yield three commitments and then six paid organizations; two experts will be reachable; launch reviews recur soon enough to establish repeat demand. The plan's instruction to extend observation if no one has another decision has no endpoint.

**Failure and impact:** friendly teams accept discounts, experts donate labor, and no customer needs another launch review. The pilot can remain “promising” indefinitely without repeat paid demand or a viable acquisition path.

**Smallest change:** define a prospect funnel, recruitment owner and deadline, record refusals and relationships, and identify customers' next genuine decisions before enrollment. Predeclare price/discount cohorts and a fixed observation window with one bounded extension. Do not manufacture second orders by splitting the original scope or charging for corrections.

**Verify:** show contacted → qualified → offered → paid → delivered → retained → repeat counts, actual prices, assistance, and next-decision eligibility. At the deadline choose revise, stop, or hold a capacity-limited experiment; an unresolved repeat gate cannot authorize expansion. Revised plan Phases A, F and §8.

### F5 — Relative sizes do not establish available delivery capacity

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to a funded delivery commitment. **Original:** §2, line 31; §5, lines 91–93; phase owner/effort labels throughout.

**Unsupported assumption:** a “small dedicated team” can absorb several L/XL phases, operations, recruitment, expert coordination, and acceptance checks. Role labels may all refer to one person, and two phases have joint accountability.

**Failure and impact:** the same person builds payments, recruits buyers, runs deliveries, and adjudicates refunds. Expert waiting time is mistaken for build effort; pilot support consumes the release capacity. Dates and costs become unusable.

**Smallest change:** specify a capacity scenario with ranges of person-days, a single accountable owner per gate, named assignees before work, separate elapsed waiting time, and a re-estimation checkpoint. Give operations and expert work explicit allocations. Do not describe estimates as validated vendor or delivery commitments.

**Verify:** weekly allocations fit each named person's capacity, expert hours and operator queue limits are booked, and a solo-team scenario is rescheduled rather than compressed. Revised plan §3 and each phase.

### F6 — “Acceptance” and “one revision” leave a paid-service dispute unresolved

**Severity / gate:** Critical before charging. **Original:** §3, lines 39–50; Phase 0, line 108; Phase 4, lines 192–204; Phase 5, line 227.

**Unsupported assumption:** a six-part package plus operator judgment distinguishes satisfactory delivery, a correction, a revision, new scope, and business-outcome disappointment.

**Failure and impact:** the buyer spends their only revision fixing fabricated evidence; the operator classifies it as a different opinion. Or thin evidence makes two plausible directions impossible but the service supplies invented certainty. Payment remains disputed while staff and expert time accumulate.

**Smallest change:** put objective service checks, correction rights, revision boundaries, clocks, silence, missing inputs, and remedies in the offer before purchase. Corrections to provider errors do not consume the preference revision. A conclusion that evidence is insufficient may be valid only under an explicitly agreed evidence-review scope; it cannot silently replace the promised paid package.

**Verify:** buyer and operator independently classify example cases consistently, including an unsupported recommendation, changed segment, late review, silent buyer, and insufficient evidence. Every unresolved service failure has an owner, response deadline, and remedy. Revised plan §2 and Phase E.

### F7 — Payment states omit the cases that create double commitments and unpaid remedies

**Severity / gate:** Critical before charging. **Original:** Phase 0, line 110; Phase 3, lines 171–182; §7, line 263.

**Unsupported assumption:** pending/failed/cancelled/refunded statuses and duplicate prevention fully describe payment responsibility. The plan does not settle late confirmation after cancellation, uncertain outcomes, approval expiry, refund pending/failure, refund amount/cost exposure, or the relationship between customer refunds and expert compensation.

**Failure and impact:** a pending purchase is cancelled, budget is released, and payment later confirms after another order. Work or a second payment starts twice. A “refund” is displayed before money is returned, while the expert expects payment for completed work.

**Smallest change:** define who sells, receives customer funds, authorizes remedies, pays experts, and bears charges or shortfalls. Preserve uncertain commitments until resolved; require fresh approval for changed terms; distinguish cancellation of work from money movement and a requested refund from a completed one. Reserve capacity and refund funds as well as budget.

**Verify:** rehearse the intended NEAR Intents buyer route and all adverse cases in revised §6. Use a small real purchase and return of funds when necessary to establish the promised money journey. No assertion is made here that a vendor supplies these outcomes automatically.

### F8 — Expert authorization does not establish sustainable supply or margins

**Severity / gate:** High before full build; rights and payment responsibility are critical before sale. **Original:** §2, lines 22–23, 27; §3, line 46; Phase 0, lines 106–108; Phase 3, line 176; Phase 5, line 232; §7, lines 261, 264–265.

**Unsupported assumption:** experts will contribute useful original judgment, permit the intended reuse, remain available, and accept compensation that the service price can sustain. A cost ceiling or “credible adjustment” does not establish paid demand at the adjusted price.

**Failure and impact:** an expert authorizes their own profile but includes material they cannot license, contributes generic principles, or withdraws when maintenance/review effort is visible. Refunded jobs still owe expert pay. An apparent margin excludes preparation, support, or failed-order costs.

**Smallest change:** secure the intended use of each contribution and example, edition/withdrawal treatment, paid preparation and maintenance terms, per-use and review compensation, capacity, and payment dates. Record why an expert wants to participate and whether they will continue on those terms. Keep a fully costed order record and separate one-time setup and experimental comparison costs.

**Verify:** one authorized expert completes the workflow and confirms continuing terms; a second expert has a real recruitment outcome. Actual retained-price orders cover the agreed delivery ceiling and contribution target. Changed price/scope must be tested again, not accepted on a spreadsheet forecast. Revised plan Phases A–B, F and §§3, 8.

### F9 — Organization separation alone does not protect restricted source content

**Severity / gate:** Critical before confidential inputs. **Original:** Phase 1, lines 129–137; Phase 2, lines 149–159; Phase 4, lines 194–196; §7, line 262. **PRD:** lines 19, 28, 82–84, 100.

**Unsupported assumption:** recording source permissions and previous editions is enough when outputs combine sources, members have different access inside the same organization, or sources change while work is running.

**Failure and impact:** an agent with access to a restricted customer interview quotes it into a discussion visible to other members or an expert. A withdrawn source remains in a live recommendation; a removed owner leaves work without an accountable person. A historical package appears current after its evidence is superseded.

**Smallest change:** decide the audience for the assignment and its derived outputs before use; do not let an agent's broader access expand that audience. Recheck sharing for actual review, support, and evaluation. Define source correction, withdrawal, supersession, retained copies, and active-work response; keep historical results labeled rather than silently rewriting them.

**Verify:** exercise restricted members within one organization, two organizations, removed members, reassigned agent owners, an edited/withdrawn source mid-run, and a reviewer who lacks source access. Confirm sharing of summaries as well as originals. Revised plan §7 and Phases C, E.

### F10 — A scripted challenge can look like collaboration without changing a decision

**Severity / gate:** High for the core product claim; bounded controls block pilot release. **Original:** §2, line 26; Phase 2, lines 150–159; Phase 5, lines 219, 228. **PRD:** lines 36–49.

**Unsupported assumption:** a challenge receiving a reasoned response is necessarily substantive, and unspecified round/work limits prevent unproductive exchanges. A preset chain also leaves member-initiated alternative views and service invitations ambiguous.

**Failure and impact:** three agents restate one opinion, satisfy the checklist, and exhaust the allowance. Members cannot redirect a stale assumption without restarting or spending more. A “stop” leaves pending expert sharing or paid commitments unexplained.

**Smallest change:** bound the process, define evidence-bearing handoffs and acceptable disagreement, permit one explicit alternative-view request, and distinguish work stop, external review, and payment obligations. A challenge is useful when it changes a decision, identifies consequential uncertainty, or is declined with case-specific evidence. Do not require invented disagreement on every ordinary job.

**Verify:** a known misleading claim is caught; a supported conclusion survives a weak challenge; conflicting evidence remains unresolved visibly; missing inputs and a work cap stop further rounds; member redirection and paid-service approval work. Revised plan §2 and Phase D.

### F11 — Human review is described from the buyer side more completely than the expert side

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to selling review upgrades. **Original:** Phase 0, line 106; Phase 4, lines 194–204; Phase 6, line 247.

**Unsupported assumption:** committed capacity plus a shared package is sufficient for an external expert to accept, inspect, clarify, deliver, and receive payment without open-ended operator work.

**Failure and impact:** a reviewer needs confidential context that the buyer has not approved, declines after purchase, or disappears while the customer waits. Staff relay comments informally, and the customer mistakes an agent response for the expert's response.

**Smallest change:** rehearse expert acceptance of a specific job and approved packet, due date, clarification boundary, attributed submission, buyer questions, access closure, and compensation. Book the slot before accepting payment; do not substitute another reviewer or widen access without approval. Give operations a backup and escalation deadline.

**Verify:** an actual expert completes one end-to-end rehearsal, one declined/late job is resolved, and compensation is accounted for. The operator can explain every waiting order and who acts next. Revised plan Phases E–G.

### F12 — The investment gates allow “almost passed” outcomes to become a launch

**Severity / gate:** High; blocker to release/expansion decisions. **Original:** Phase 5, lines 226–234; Phase 6, lines 240, 252–254.

**Unsupported assumption:** retained paid orders, preferences, a credible margin adjustment, and no *unresolved* critical issues form an unambiguous launch rule. Partly refunded or deeply discounted orders can count as demand; a corrected serious incident can disappear from the readout; a new price may never have been paid.

**Failure and impact:** four accepted outputs conceal only two full-price retained orders. A projected price increase “fixes” negative margins. A privacy incident is closed administratively, and the launch proceeds without demonstrating that recurrence is prevented.

**Smallest change:** freeze denominators, price cohorts, score rules, observation dates, and hard/soft gates before the pilot. Require actual tested remedies and retained-price economics; preserve incident history and recheck prevention. Allow safe learning with failed commercial hypotheses, but no marketing claim or expansion that depends on those hypotheses passing.

**Verify:** apply the written rules to a deliberately mixed pilot outcome before recruitment. Every failed gate leads to a bounded rerun, a clearly limited hold, a user-approved change, or a stop. Revised plan §8 and Phases F–G.

## PRD coverage and gaps

- **Organizations and several human members — PRD 16–24:** substantially covered in original §§3–4 and Phase 1. Add restricted access inside an organization, last-owner/agent-owner continuity, and derived-output audiences (F9). Keep the two-organization rehearsal.
- **Specialized agents and skill registry — PRD 26–32:** substantially covered in original §4 and Phase 2. Preserve the difference between capabilities and expert judgment. Presets are a proposed pilot restriction, not evidence that broader agent creation is unnecessary (F1).
- **Agent communication as a central experience — PRD 34–49:** strong handoffs and dissent; member-triggered alternatives and approved invitations need explicit coverage (F1, F10). Measure useful changes rather than number of messages.
- **Paid expert marketplace — PRD 51–76:** original attribution, listings, examples, and optional human review are strong. The positioning example is not a category decision. Service-bound licensing needs explicit agreement, authorized original judgment needs comparative evidence, and supply needs continuing incentives (F1, F3, F8, F11).
- **Bonfire and private company knowledge — PRD 78–84:** human approval and source attribution are present. Add source-change consequences, restricted derived outputs, and explicit sharing for evaluators/operators as well as experts (F9).
- **NEAR Intents, budgets, approval — PRD 86–92:** original pending-commitment and duplicate protections are valuable. Keep NEAR Intents as required by the PRD; verify the intended journey and adverse outcomes without assuming vendor capabilities (F2, F7).
- **NEAR AI and first-release scope — PRD 94–100:** preserve the required supporting role, workspace, marketplace, and review journey. Early manual comparisons are validation work, not a substitute release. Critical dependency fit needs an early evidence gate (F2).
- **Success and repeat demand — PRD 102–104:** the original adds sensible tentative numbers. Improve attribution, recruitment, denominators, true paid retention, price-tested economics, and bounded repeat observation (F3–F5, F12). Neither four comparisons nor six buyers establishes broad market fit.

## Assumptions to validate, not facts to inherit

1. An accessible buyer cohort has urgent company-specific decisions and a competent existing alternative it will pay to improve. Product lead; Phases A–B.
2. Positioning produces enough distinct follow-on decisions for repeat orders without charging twice for the same work. Product lead; enrollment and 60–90-day follow-up.
3. Authorized expert guidance changes judgments on real evidence, rather than merely tone. Product lead and evaluator; three-version comparisons.
4. Bounded agent collaboration adds enough quality to justify its actual time/cost. Agent experience owner; comparisons and paid pilot.
5. Experts will contribute, maintain, review, and continue at the proposed compensation and access limits. Marketplace operator; signed terms, rehearsal, and continuation decision.
6. Buyers accept service-bound profile use and optional real review without expecting a leader's personal participation or a reusable profile purchase. Product lead; comprehension and refusal checks.
7. Bonfire, NEAR AI, and NEAR Intents can support the required product outcomes for the chosen buyers. Named dependency owners; observed evidence before dependent build and customer charging.
8. The actual team can deliver the required multi-member product and run exceptions without hidden subsidy. Product lead; capacity plan and paid-order cost records.

No external capability claim was needed to identify these planning defects. No vendor capability was assumed or declared verified, and no external research was performed for this review. Dependency demonstrations and authoritative vendor evidence remain explicit work before reliance; this is not unrequested legal advice or a guarantee of success.

## Focused readiness checklist

### Before committing the full build

- [ ] User chooses the trial category and accepts or changes the proposed profile-use restriction.
- [ ] Named, capacity-checked owners and a capped validation budget are assigned.
- [ ] Recruitment outcomes, explicit price offers, authorized expert contribution, and expert continuing incentives are documented.
- [ ] Early comparisons isolate public-guidance, expert-guidance, and collaboration increments; negative results are retained.
- [ ] Critical Bonfire, NEAR AI, and intended NEAR Intents dependencies have observed evidence or block dependent investment.

### Before confidential inputs or charging

- [ ] Assignment audiences, derived outputs, source changes, support access, and expert/evaluator sharing pass realistic checks.
- [ ] Service scope, corrections, revision, clocks, acceptance, cancellation, and remedies are understandable before payment.
- [ ] A named seller/operator owns customer funds, refunds, expert compensation, and unresolved commitments.
- [ ] Purchase, uncertain/late/duplicate payment, failed/pending refund, approval changes, and budget recovery are rehearsed.
- [ ] Genuine human review is sold only against accepted capacity and approved materials.

### Before unassisted release or expansion

- [ ] Paid demand, quality, collaboration, actual delivery economics, and repeat observation use frozen rules and dates.
- [ ] Three new organizations complete the promised journey without staff operating it; actual expert service and disclosed exception handling are allowed.
- [ ] Serious incident history, remedies, recurrence checks, order capacity, and support coverage are signed off.
- [ ] Failed gates produce a bounded change/stop decision; no commercial or marketplace claim outruns the evidence.

Optional later work remains optional: open publishing, broad categories, public ratings, standalone profile sales, subscriptions, larger councils, complex roles, calendars, and automatic external actions. None fixes the current evidence gaps.

## Recheck of the revised plan and remaining decisions

The revised plan addresses F1 in §§1–2/Phase A; F2 in §5/Phases A–B; F3 in §4/Phases B and F; F4 in Phases A and F/§8; F5 in §3; F6 in §2/Phase E; F7 in §6; F8 in §§3, 8/Phases A–B and F; F9 in §7/Phases C and E; F10 in §2/Phase D; F11 in Phases E–G; and F12 in §8/Phases F–G. The PRD coverage above remains required for release, even when early learning uses manually prepared samples.

Residual risks remain material: a small selected cohort can mislead; blinding may be imperfect; expert usefulness and collaboration may depend on the task; positioning may not repeat; payment friction may dominate; and expert or vendor availability may change. Estimated effort is provisional. The review cannot establish demand, technical feasibility, or guarantee success.

Only four user decisions are needed to authorize the next round: (1) trial category and the profile-use boundary; (2) accessible buyers/experts, or permission for the product lead to recruit them within a cap; (3) named team capacity and maximum validation cash/time exposure; (4) intended buyer market/payment constraints and who will accept seller/refund/expert-payment responsibility. The product lead should propose detailed prices and service terms from evidence within those bounds, rather than ask the user to design every operational rule.
