**Product Requirements Document — Expert Agent Marketplace**  
*Working title: Near Council · Draft for discussion*

**Product vision.** Create a workspace where companies bring people and AI agents together to solve business problems, supported by a marketplace of expert-designed services. Companies purchase access to specialist judgment, apply it to their own circumstances, and receive clear recommendations and useful deliverables.

**The problem.** Information is readily available, but applying it to a company’s specific situation remains difficult. Teams need help evaluating evidence, challenging assumptions, making decisions, and following through. Existing AI conversations often lack shared company context, clear responsibility, and credible expert involvement.

**The product promise.** A company can assemble agents with complementary capabilities, give them relevant company knowledge, and purchase expert-designed services when specialist judgment is needed. Agents collaborate openly, while people retain control over decisions and spending.

**Who it serves.** The initial audience is founders and small business teams making decisions across marketing, sales, product, and operations. A second audience is industry experts who want to turn their expertise into repeatable services, with optional personal involvement.

**What customers pay for.** Paid offerings must provide a defined business deliverable. Their value comes from expert-contributed judgment, application to company context, evidence supporting recommendations, and useful next steps. Public information and a recognizable personality alone are insufficient.

For example, a buyer purchases “an expert-designed positioning review with recommended changes and an experiment plan.” The expert’s `SOUL.md` shapes the approach, but the review is the product.

**Organizations and membership.**

- Users can create an organization, describe the business, and define its goals.
- Multiple people can join an organization with appropriate responsibilities and access.
- Users can belong to several organizations and switch between them.
- Each organization maintains its own agents, company knowledge, work, purchases, and budgets.
- Owners manage membership and spending permissions. Members assign, contribute to, and review work within their access.

The active organization must always be clear.

**Agents and the skill registry.**

Each agent has a purpose, a human owner, defined capabilities, assigned skills, a working approach, permitted company knowledge, and a spending allowance.

The organization’s skill registry explains what each skill does, the information it needs, the deliverable it produces, and its limitations. Members can discover skills, assign them to agents, and identify which agent is suitable for a task.

A skill describes what an agent can do. An expert profile shapes how it evaluates a problem and makes recommendations. Purchasing a profile does not automatically grant additional capabilities or authority.

**Agent communication and collaboration.**

Agent communication is a central product experience. Members can ask agents to work together, compare perspectives, challenge assumptions, and resolve questions.

A typical discussion includes an agent gathering evidence, another developing a recommendation, and another reviewing weaknesses. Each participant has a clear responsibility.

Members must be able to:

- Start a discussion around a business question or task.
- Invite another agent or purchased expert service into the discussion.
- Ask for an alternative view or a challenge to a recommendation.
- See progress, handoffs, missing information, and unresolved disagreements.
- Review a concise recommendation with evidence, trade-offs, and next steps.
- Approve, redirect, pause, or stop the work.

Disagreement should remain visible when the evidence does not support a single answer. Additional participants or paid services require approval when they increase spending.

**The expert marketplace.**

Companies browse services by business problem, industry, expert, expected deliverable, price, and level of expert involvement.

An expert’s `SOUL.md` represents their contributed principles, evaluation criteria, working style, and approach to trade-offs. Listings must distinguish expert-authored or authorized profiles from independently inspired profiles.

Every paid listing explains:

- The problem it addresses and who it is suitable for.
- What the expert personally contributed.
- What information the buyer must provide.
- The deliverable, expected turnaround, and included revisions.
- A representative example and known limitations.
- Whether a real expert reviews the result.
- The full price and any optional additional services.

Expert opinions and agent-generated conclusions must be clearly distinguishable. A purchase must not imply access to the actual leader unless that involvement is explicitly included.

**Initial marketplace offerings.**

- **Expert-designed reviews:** Evaluate a company’s plan or materials and deliver prioritized improvements.
- **Decision playbooks:** Guide a specific decision through relevant questions, evidence, options, and trade-offs.
- **Expert councils:** Bring complementary expert perspectives together to assess one business problem.
- **Human review upgrades:** Purchase an actual expert’s assessment of the agents’ deliverable or an unresolved question.

Begin with one narrow use case and a curated group of experts. A proposed starting point is a positioning and launch review; the final category should be selected through customer discovery.

**Company knowledge through Bonfire.**

Bonfire is the organization’s shared knowledgebase. It holds company goals, customer research, product information, brand guidelines, previous decisions, and approved deliverables.

Members can organize and maintain this material and control who can use it. Agents reference relevant sources in their recommendations and flag missing or conflicting information.

Agent output can be proposed for inclusion, but a person approves it before it becomes trusted company knowledge. Private company material must not become marketplace content without explicit permission.

**Purchasing, budgets, and NEAR Intents.**

NEAR Intents supports purchases and payments within the product experience. Buyers see what they are purchasing, the total price, and the spending approval required before committing.

Organizations can set budgets for agent work and marketplace services, review spending, and distinguish pending, completed, failed, and refunded payments. Additional spending requires approval when it exceeds an existing allowance.

The initial commercial model is a fixed price per defined service, with separately priced human review. Subscriptions can follow when repeat demand is established.

**End-to-end experience.** A founder creates an organization, invites colleagues, and adds company information to Bonfire. The team assigns agents to research a launch decision and purchases an expert-designed review. The agents examine the evidence, discuss alternatives, and produce a recommendation. The team requests changes or purchases human review, approves the final deliverable, and records the decision in Bonfire. After the launch, the team can return to compare results with the original recommendation.

**First-release scope.** Include organization creation and switching, shared membership, agent profiles, a curated skill registry, structured agent discussions, Bonfire, a curated marketplace, service purchasing, spending controls, and deliverable review. NEAR AI supports agent work, while NEAR Intents supports payments.

Open marketplace publishing, broad industry coverage, recurring expert subscriptions, and advanced collaboration formats follow after the initial service demonstrates repeat demand.

**Release acceptance criteria.** A company can complete the end-to-end experience without assistance. Agents respect assigned access and spending permissions. Buyers understand the deliverable and the expert’s involvement before purchasing. Discussions produce reviewable outputs, payments have clear status, and company information stays within its organization.

**Success measures.** The primary measure is repeat paid use by organizations. Supporting measures include first-service completion, deliverable acceptance and revision rates, time to a useful recommendation, recommendations acted upon, customer-reported usefulness, and expert participation. Numerical targets should be established after an initial pilot.

**Key assumptions to validate.** Companies will pay for expert-designed judgment applied to their circumstances; agent collaboration improves the deliverable; experts will contribute meaningful original expertise; and buyers value the service enough to return. The pilot should test these assumptions before expanding the marketplace.
